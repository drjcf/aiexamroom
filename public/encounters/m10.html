AI in the Exam Room Provider Module 10
Module 10 · The legal and ethical ground

You sign the chart. The machine signs nothing. This is the ground that asymmetry leaves you standing on.

Two kinds of AI, two kinds of governance, and one accountable human at the point of care. What the law requires of you now, what ethics asks beyond the law, and how to govern a tool that can be fluent and wrong at the same time.

Before you start · read this once
Not legal advice

This module is educational. The law here is jurisdiction-specific and moving fast; the dates below are current as of mid-2026 and several are in active flux. Verify the current rules, and your own malpractice and liability coverage, with counsel and your carrier before relying on any of it.

The first distinction · what kind of AI is this
Two kinds of AI. Two kinds of governance.

Most confusion about AI law and ethics comes from collapsing two different things into one word. Before anything else, decide which one is in front of you, because nearly every duty below lands harder on the second.

Healthcare AI · population level

Scheduling, coding, documentation drafting, cohort analytics, prior-authorization triage. Errors are diffuse, statistical, and usually recoverable. It can tolerate deploy-and-monitor, so governance can be largely retrospective.

Medical AI · the individual encounter

Output that bears on one patient's diagnosis, treatment, or decision. Tolerance for added error is effectively zero. The right posture is prospective: validation and credentialing gates cleared before deployment, not after harm.

The framework · watch the drift

A tool that drafts your note today and suggests a differential tomorrow has crossed from Healthcare AI into Medical AI, often without crossing any regulatory line. The clinician inherits that gap.

The legal ground · what the law requires now
The law still runs through you.

No jurisdiction has moved ultimate clinical accountability onto an algorithm. You remain the accountable human, and the law is now scrutinizing not just your decision but how you used the tool.

Know the tool's regulatory status.

In the US the FDA regulates AI-enabled device functions, and its 2025 guidance lets vendors pre-clear specified model updates without a new submission. The trap: most conversational and generative tools in clinics are not cleared devices at all. They enter as non-device decision support or general software, with no premarket review for any clinical task. "FDA cleared" for one indication does not cover off-label use, and "not a device" does not mean safe.

The standard of care is still yours, and so is the liability.

The test remains the reasonable physician of your specialty. Following an AI does not relieve that duty; rely on it and you can still be liable if the action falls below standard. What is new is that the analysis now weighs whether use was on or off label, the stakes, and whether you documented your reasoning. This is the Cosigner Problem: you are positioned as the liability-bearing reviewer of a recommendation you did not generate and cannot fully inspect. The law treats cosigning as deciding.

Disclosure law is arriving unevenly.

Texas's TRAIGA, effective January 1, 2026, requires healthcare providers to disclose AI use in care. Utah requires disclosing generative AI on request, Colorado's act was scaled back in 2026, several states added chatbot rules, and one moved to ban AI therapy bots. No federal preemption passed. Default to disclosing when AI touches a decision, and document it.

Privacy is easy to breach by reflex.

Protected health information fed to a vendor tool needs a Business Associate Agreement; pasting it into a consumer chatbot is a breach risk, not a shortcut. Confirm where the data goes, and who may train on it, before the first encounter.

Thread · the Accountability Asymmetry, now in your policy

You carry malpractice insurance and a revocable license. The AI company carries disclaimers. And as of January 1, 2026, ISO endorsements CG 40 47 and CG 40 48 let your carrier exclude generative-AI harms from liability coverage entirely, with strong uptake and similar language spreading into errors-and-omissions and directors-and-officers lines. Read your renewal for "artificial intelligence," "generative," and "algorithm." The coverage you assume may be written out from under you.

The ethical ground · what ethics asks beyond the law
Four old duties, and where each one bends.

The law sets the floor. The four principles set the rest, and clinical AI bends each in a specific place.

Autonomy: the patient's interest in knowing.

Where AI materially shapes a diagnosis or treatment, the case for disclosure and shared decision-making grows with the stakes and the opacity. A recommendation you cannot interpret is one you cannot consent a patient to, which is why explainability is the enabling condition, not a nicety.

Beneficence · the evidence under the boundary

Ceiling on the licensing exam. Far below passing on the specialty boards.

Systems that score at the ceiling on licensing-style exams drop sharply on specialty board-level questions in published benchmarks, often well below a passing mark. The failure is reasoning, not retrieval: having the source material is not the same as reasoning through it. Call the limit the Cognitive Altitude Boundary, the level of synthesis above which current systems become unreliable. Set deployment standards at board-level competence under real conditions, not at licensing-exam performance. And design around automation bias and the sensing gap, since the machine cannot see the color, the breathing, or the fear that never reaches the text.

Thread · justice and equity

AI inherits the inequities of its training data and underperforms on populations it was not built on. A tool validated in a well-resourced center can transfer harm to the patients least able to absorb it. Ask who it was trained and validated on, monitor for differential performance, and never assume a system built for one setting is safe in another.

The framework · Intelligent Humility

The counterweight to confident error is a system built to say "I do not know," to communicate calibrated uncertainty, and to decline rather than confabulate outside its validated knowledge. A system that never expresses uncertainty is not more capable; it is less trustworthy, because it has removed the signal you need to calibrate reliance.

The human trap

Two tendencies undermine all of the above. The Effort Asymmetry is the pull toward the easiest survivable path: accepting the output is faster than interrogating it, and that gradient compounds across a clinic. And a clinician who reviews without truly deciding has taken the liability of authorship without doing the work of authorship. Neither is fixed by willpower; both are fixed by design and documentation.

The practical layer · how to govern it
Govern it like you mean it.

Principles become safe only as practice. Three checklists and one control rule.

Take this with you

Before adoption

  • Get the tool's regulatory status and exact intended use in writing.
  • Ask who it was trained and validated on, and for evidence at the relevant clinical altitude.
  • Be wary of any tool whose developer will not disclose how it works.
  • Confirm the data flow and the governing agreement.
  • Check your liability coverage for a new generative-AI exclusion.
Take this with you

At the point of care

  • Apply the standard of care independently; treat AI as confirmatory, not the decision.
  • Be most deliberate in high-stakes and off-label use.
  • Watch for automation bias in yourself.
  • Document what the tool contributed, how your assessment compared, and any departure.
  • Disclose AI involvement to the patient when it touches the decision.
Take this with you

At the organization

  • Stand up a governance function; do not leave adoption to individuals.
  • Keep an inventory of deployed tools and their intended uses.
  • Define validation, disclosure, consent, training, and audit duties.
  • Build monitoring for differential performance and a real incident path.
The framework · Execution-Bound Governance

For higher-stakes deployment, build the control plane so no consequential action proceeds without a valid Decision Record, and make it fail-closed: the default without a valid record is to stop, not proceed. A Decision Record names the consequence owner, ties to a revocable credential, and produces the artifact an independent reviewer can audit later. A failure becomes a documented asset, not an untraceable event.

The open questions · where the law still lags
What still has no answer.

These are the gaps governance proposals should target, because the law has not closed them.

Who is liable when the developer is in the loop?

Allocation among physician, health system, and developer is unsettled, and currently defaults onto the physician.

Who can revoke an autonomous system's authority?

Proposals to license autonomous clinical AI run ahead of any framework for withdrawing that authority for cause.

Who insures the harm the technology creates?

The new exclusions threaten to leave clinicians and developers uninsured for exactly the harms AI introduces.

Whose disclosure rule applies?

A multi-state practice cannot satisfy the patchwork by intuition, and global harmonization barely exists.

The throughline

Every one of these is the Accountability Asymmetry. Until consequence ownership, credential revocability, and independent adjudication attach to the systems shaping the decision, the human at the bedside keeps absorbing the risk of decisions they did not make.

The instrument can be brilliant and blind at once. You are the one who can tell the difference, and the only one the law, the patient, and the consequence can find.

Know which kind of AI you are holding. Keep the judgment, the senses, the decision, and the name on the chart. Govern the rest like it can be confidently wrong, because it can. Optimize the clinician. You cannot replace them.
