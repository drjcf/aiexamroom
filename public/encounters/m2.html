Module 02 · But AI said…

The doctor wrote the prescription. The app wrote back.

The same tool that frightens people in one room reassures them in the next, and it sounds equally certain both times. When the AI questions the plan, the answer can't be who holds the louder credential. The same encounter, from all four seats in the room.

Dee: 8 days of sinus pressure and congestion, low fever earlier, getting better now. My doctor wants to give me azithromycin. Do I need it?

AI: Most acute sinus infections are viral, and antibiotics usually don't help in the first ~10 days — especially once you're already improving. Watchful waiting and symptom relief are often the first-line approach.

Dee: Anything I should ask about?

AI: Azithromycin can affect heart rhythm and may interact with other medications that do the same, so tell your doctor everything you take and ask them to check. One caveat: I can't examine you and this isn't a diagnosis — your clinician can see things I can't.

Measured, mostly correct — and it even flagged its own limits.

The dissection · the truth of the encounter, for every seat
What this was actually about.
Thread · accountability

The AI dispensed advice it carries no liability for. If Dee skips an antibiotic she needed and worsens, or takes one that harms her, the consequence and the chart entry belong to the clinician, not the app. That is exactly why authority can't be the answer to "but the app said": the person who owns the outcome has to engage the reasoning, not pull rank over it. Authority without engagement is liability without the trust that makes it bearable.

Thread · equity

Who gets to fact-check their doctor? It takes confidence, language, and a clinician willing to be questioned. A patient who fears being labeled "difficult," or who can't read the AI's English, won't raise the point even when the AI is right. The same tool that empowers the assertive patient silences the deferential one.

The framework

This is AI as the confident third voice in the room. Its hazard isn't accuracy; it's that it speaks with identical certainty whether it's right or wrong, and the patient can't tell which. The clinician's job is to be the one who can: credit the true points, correct the false ones, and never settle it by who has the louder credential.
